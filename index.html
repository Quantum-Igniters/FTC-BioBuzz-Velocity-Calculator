Robot Inputs

Check your motor specifications on the manufacturer's website for the correct encoder ticks per revolution. Example: a goBILDA 6000 RPM motor uses 28 ticks/rev.
Straight shooter — dual powered flywheels
This matches the straight shooter in your sketch: the ball passes between two powered flywheels. With both wheel surfaces moving toward the exit at the same speed, the ideal ball exit speed is approximately the flywheel surface speed. Shooter C2C is used to calculate the physical gap and ball compression.
Curved compression hood calculation
This matches the curved shooter in your sketch: the same two powered flywheels are used, but the hood follows a curved path around the ball. The curved hood keeps the ball constrained and compressed over a longer contact path before release. Enter the actual ball compression in millimeters. The ball exits tangent to the curved hood. Required hood wrap = required exit angle − entry tangent angle, and contact length = hood radius × wrap angle in radians.
Ball sizes are fixed: Nectar = 3.6 in, Pollen = 2.8 in. Target height is fixed at 60 in. No transfer-efficiency input is used.

Nectar Pollen Shooter Hive
Physics model: both shooter types use the same projectile-motion calculation for the required ball exit speed. The straight shooter uses a straight compression path. The constant-compression curved shooter keeps the hood concentric with the flywheel, so the physical gap stays equal to the compressed ball thickness through the arc and the ball exits tangent to that arc. The curved geometry can improve consistency and energy transfer, but the exact real-world RPM improvement depends on friction, wheel material, slip, and ball deformation, so final RPM should be calibrated on the robot.
